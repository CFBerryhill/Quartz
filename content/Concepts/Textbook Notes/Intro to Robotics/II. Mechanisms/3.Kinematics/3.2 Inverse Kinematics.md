$q$ describes a unique joint configuration. Forward kinematics uses $q$ to find the end effector pose. The goal of Inverse Kinematics (IK) is to find a joint configuration that results in a desired end effector pose. i.e.

$$q = f^{-1}(r)$$
For a mobile robot, we also need to consider local velocities. 

### 3.2.1 Solvability
If $n$ - or the number of [[2.3 Degrees of Freedom|DoF]] of joint space is less than $m$ - the number of DoF of task space then there may be poses in task space that are not in joint space, creating an infeasible situation. 

### 3.2.2 IK of a Simple Manipulator arm

Consider this robot arm:
![[RobotArm.png]]
We need to solve for $q=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha \\ \beta\end{bmatrix}$

Consider only the first link, $l_1$. Solving for $\alpha$ yields two solutions:

$$\alpha = +/-cos^{-1}\frac{x_{1}}{l_{1}}$$
one above the table and one below. 

Solving even just for $\alpha, \beta$ becomes cumbersome. Is there a scalable solution?

If we specify not only the position but also the orientation, then the desired pose will have the form
$$\begin{bmatrix} cos\theta & -sin\theta & 0 & x \\ sin\theta &  cos\theta &  0 &  y \\ 0 &  0 &  1 &  0 \\ 0 &  0 &  0 &  1\end{bmatrix}$$
we can simplify  this, yet this still does not scale well.

Let's use optimization. You can define the euclidian distance between the current pose and the goal pose as a function of $\alpha, \beta$, then minimize. This will find a local minima.
